The problem

Prediction markets stop where the big leagues stop.

No markets

Niche sports go unlisted

On race weekend for UCI Downhill World Cup Round 8 (Whistler, 26 Sep 2026), Polymarket had zero markets for "downhill", "Whistler DH", "Crankworx" or "UCI downhill".

No pricing

Exchanges won't list what they can't price

Listing a market needs someone to make an opening price and hold inventory. For niche sports no one has the model, the data pipeline, or the settlement feed.

Wasted knowledge

The people with an edge have no venue

Fans, team staff, media and data nerds know these sports deeply. Today their only outlet is private bets between friends.

The insight

The bottleneck is liquidity, and liquidity comes from pricing.

Market makers need three things

  • A fair price they trust, updated as the weekend unfolds
  • Risk tools: spread, size caps, exposure and P&L
  • Clean settlement from official results

All three come from data and software

Timed sports publish rich official data: every run and every split, with DNFs, DSQs and qualifying structure. With a proper pipeline and model, anyone can become a market maker in a sport they know.

League partners from day one

Leagues should want us in the room early.

What a league gets (e.g. the UCI and the World Series organiser)
  • Integrity monitoring: we see every quote and every bet, so we can flag suspicious patterns and share alerts with the league
  • A say in the market list: which markets are offered, and hard limits such as no markets on junior (under-18) categories
  • New revenue: a share of data, oracle and exchange revenue from their sport
  • Fan engagement: live odds and "who makes the Final" stories for broadcasts and social media
What we watch for
  • Money arriving just before withdrawals, start-list changes or injury news
  • Bets on outcomes a rider or team controls, such as a rider not making the Final, a DNF or a DNS
  • Price moves that public information (training, qualifying) doesn't explain
  • Bets that are unusually large for the account, or clusters of linked accounts
  • Market design that removes the easiest targets before they're listed

🔁 One playbook, every new league

Each new league gets the same package: official data agreement → approved market list → integrity dashboard and alerts → revenue share. The integrity checks work on our shared multi-sport schema, so they apply to any league we add without new code.

What we get: official data rights (instead of pulling data ourselves), earlier start lists and withdrawals, and credibility with regulators and licensed exchanges.

Local racing leagues

Grassroots series want the podium to matter more.

Regional cups, club races and weeknight series run hundreds of events a year. They have passionate communities, sponsors looking for reach, and no way to put stakes on the standings.

🏆 Pick-the-podium contests

Free-to-enter predictions on each race and on the season standings. Scores are weighted by our odds, so calling an underdog pays off, and there's a live leaderboard with sponsor prizes.

💸 Sponsor podium bounties

Sponsors fund bonus money for podium results, and our odds size it fairly: a bigger bounty for an underdog podium, a smaller one for the favourite. The league gets a new product to sell to sponsors.

⏱️ League dashboard

Local timing results go into the same multi-sport schema: standings, rider histories, "who makes the podium" odds, and integrity alerts for organisers.

Guardrails
  • No real-money wagering, except through licensed partners
  • Adults only. Riders, officials and their families can't enter contests on their own races
  • Organisers approve every contest and see our integrity alerts
Why it matters to us
  • A long tail of events means more data, more community, and a better model
  • Every local league runs the same playbook, and doesn't need new code
  • A funnel of riders, fans and sponsors toward the bigger leagues

First beta

A friends-and-family test on a real race weekend.

How it runs
  • Seeded through a local trendsetter's own network: they personally invite people they know. No public posts, ads or open link
  • Invite-only and capped: a small, named list of participants for the weekend
  • We quote YES/NO on win, podium, make-the-Final and championship moves from the model, with a set spread
  • Participants take positions over the weekend. Every quote and bet is logged in the house book
  • Markets settle from official timing after the Final. Money is handled among participants, outside the app
  • No rake: this tests the product; it isn't a business yet
What it proves
  • A trusted local voice can bring in real participants through word of mouth alone
  • People understand and act on YES/NO prices for riders
  • How takers behave against a spread, and which market types they want
  • The quote → bet → settle loop and the risk dashboard work under real use
  • Model prices against real flow: where we get picked off, and where we don't
What it isn't

It isn't a public launch. The group stays closed and there's no rake; we'll confirm the setup with counsel before running it. Wider access comes through licensed partners (Phase 2), not by opening the house book to the public.

The tooling for this already exists: the house book, quote sheet, bet log, exposure and EV view, and automatic settlement for race markets. The Whistler 2026 book (75 markets) was generated with it.

The hard part, up front

A clone is a licensing project before it's a product.

Reality
  • In the US, event-contract exchanges operate as CFTC-regulated markets. Getting there took Kalshi years; Polymarket's US return came through buying an already-licensed exchange.
  • Sports event contracts are actively contested by US state gaming regulators.
  • Elsewhere, including Canada's provinces, taking bets from the public is usually licensed gambling.
  • Official timing data has usage terms. A commercial product needs data licences from timing providers and series organisers.
Our approach
  • Start business-to-business: sell data, pricing and tools, and hold no customer funds.
  • Reach users through licensed partners before running our own venue.
  • Get licensing counsel and data agreements early, as Phase 1 milestones.
  • Pick launch markets by legal clarity, not by size.
  • Partner with leagues for official data and integrity monitoring, and never list junior-category markets.

This is a summary for planning, not legal advice. The regulatory picture changes fast and differs by jurisdiction.
